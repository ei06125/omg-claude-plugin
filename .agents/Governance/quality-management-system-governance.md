# Quality Management System Governance Guide

Last reviewed: 2026-09-19

This guide provides a practical baseline for establishing, operating, evaluating, and improving a Quality Management System (QMS). It applies to the organisation as a whole, including product development, service delivery, customer support, procurement, security, privacy, and software engineering. Detailed software-testing practices remain in [software-quality-testing-governance.md](software-quality-testing-governance.md).

## 1. Purpose of a QMS

A QMS is the coordinated set of policies, objectives, processes, responsibilities, resources, controls, records, and improvement mechanisms used to deliver products and services consistently and satisfy customer, statutory, regulatory, and contractual requirements.

It should help the company:

- Understand customer and other relevant interested-party needs.
- Translate strategy into measurable quality objectives.
- Define and control the processes that create value.
- Prevent nonconformities and reduce variation and waste.
- Detect problems, correct them, and address their causes.
- Make decisions using reliable evidence.
- Retain organisational knowledge and remain resilient to change.
- Improve customer satisfaction and long-term performance.

A QMS is not a collection of documents produced for an audit. Documentation should support reliable work, accountability, evidence, and improvement.

## 2. ISO 9000 family and related standards

### ISO 9000:2026

ISO 9000 defines QMS fundamentals, concepts, principles, and vocabulary. Use it to establish a common language and avoid inconsistent meanings for terms such as quality, process, requirement, nonconformity, correction, corrective action, and audit. ISO 9000 is guidance and vocabulary, not a certification standard.

Source: [ISO — ISO 9000:2026](https://www.iso.org/standard/9000)

### ISO 9001:2026

ISO 9001 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving a QMS. The 2026 edition replaced ISO 9001:2015 on 2026-09-16. It retains the harmonised management-system structure and places renewed emphasis on leadership, quality culture, ethical behaviour, strategic alignment, and distinct treatment of risks and opportunities.

ISO 9001 is the certifiable standard in the ISO 9000 family, but certification is optional unless a law, contract, customer, or business objective requires it. A certificate indicates that an accredited or otherwise selected certification body assessed conformity within a defined scope; it does not guarantee that every product is defect-free.

Source: [ISO — ISO 9001:2026](https://www.iso.org/standard/9001)

### ISO 9004:2018

ISO 9004 provides guidance for improving the quality of the organisation and achieving sustained success beyond minimum conformity. It includes a self-assessment approach and is not intended for certification.

Source: [ISO — ISO 9004:2018](https://www.iso.org/standard/70397.html)

### ISO 19011:2026

ISO 19011 provides guidance on audit principles, managing audit programmes, conducting management-system audits, and evaluating auditor competence. It supports first-party internal audits, second-party supplier audits, and external audit programmes. It is guidance and is not independently certifiable.

Source: [ISO — ISO 19011:2026](https://www.iso.org/standard/19011)

### Supporting standards

Select only those that address a real need:

- **ISO 10005:** guidance for quality plans.
- **ISO 10006:** quality management in projects.
- **ISO 10007:** configuration management.
- **ISO 10012:** measurement-management systems.
- **ISO 10013:** documented information guidance.
- **ISO 10014:** realising financial and economic benefits through quality management.
- **ISO 10015:** competence management and people development.
- **ISO 10018:** people engagement.
- **ISO 10019:** selection and use of QMS consultants.
- **ISO 10001–10004:** customer satisfaction, complaints, dispute resolution, and monitoring.
- **ISO/IEC 25000 SQuaRE family:** software and systems quality requirements, models, measurement, and evaluation.

Verify the current edition and applicability before adopting a standard. Purchase or obtain licensed access to normative ISO text where conformity is required; summaries are not substitutes for the standard.

## 3. Quality management principles

The ISO 9000 family is based on seven quality management principles:

1. **Customer focus:** understand current and future needs, meet requirements, and seek to exceed expectations where appropriate.
2. **Leadership:** establish unity of purpose, direction, culture, and conditions in which people can contribute.
3. **Engagement of people:** develop competent, empowered, and engaged people at all levels.
4. **Process approach:** manage related activities as interacting processes within a coherent system.
5. **Improvement:** maintain a continuing focus on improvement and adaptation.
6. **Evidence-based decision making:** use reliable analysis while recognising uncertainty and limitations.
7. **Relationship management:** manage relationships with suppliers, partners, customers, and other interested parties that affect sustained success.

Source: [ISO — Quality management principles](https://www.iso.org/iso/pub100080.pdf)

These are decision principles, not slogans. Translate them into responsibilities, process controls, measures, reviews, and improvement work.

## 4. Context and QMS scope

Determine the internal and external issues that can affect quality objectives. Consider strategy, market, technology, regulation, customer expectations, workforce, organisational knowledge, supply chain, security, privacy, resilience, climate-related relevance, and economic conditions.

Identify relevant interested parties and their requirements, including:

- Customers and end users.
- Employees and contractors.
- Owners, investors, and governing bodies.
- Regulators and certification bodies.
- Suppliers, subprocessors, distributors, and partners.
- Communities and other affected parties.

Define the QMS scope by products, services, processes, sites, legal entities, and exclusions. The scope must reflect actual operations and interfaces; it should not be narrowed merely to avoid difficult requirements.

Maintain a concise scope statement and review it after material organisational, product, location, regulatory, or supplier changes.

## 5. Process architecture

Map the organisation as a system of interacting processes rather than isolated departments.

For each material process, define:

- Purpose and intended outputs.
- Owner and participants.
- Customers and other interested parties.
- Inputs, suppliers, activities, outputs, and downstream processes.
- Applicable requirements and acceptance criteria.
- Risks, opportunities, and controls.
- Resources, competence, tools, and infrastructure.
- Measures and review cadence.
- Required records and retained knowledge.
- Escalation, exception, and improvement paths.

Typical process groups include:

- Strategy and governance.
- Sales, contracting, and customer onboarding.
- Product and service design and development.
- Procurement and supplier management.
- Service delivery and operations.
- Customer support and complaint handling.
- People, competence, and organisational knowledge.
- Security, privacy, risk, and compliance.
- Measurement, audit, corrective action, and improvement.

Identify handoffs and feedback loops. Many quality failures occur between processes even when individual teams follow their own procedures.

## 6. Leadership and quality culture

Top management remains accountable for QMS effectiveness even when operational work is delegated.

Leadership should:

- Align the QMS with strategy and business processes.
- Establish the quality policy and measurable objectives.
- Ensure requirements are understood and met.
- Assign authorities and provide resources.
- Promote customer focus, ethical behaviour, learning, and speaking up.
- Remove incentives that reward speed or output while hiding quality risk.
- Review performance and act on systemic weaknesses.
- Support process owners and protect auditor independence.

Quality culture is visible in everyday decisions: whether bad news travels quickly, whether people can stop unsafe or nonconforming work, whether lessons are shared, and whether leaders address causes rather than blame individuals.

## 7. Quality policy

The quality policy should be short, relevant, and usable. It should:

- Fit the organisation’s purpose, context, and strategic direction.
- Provide a framework for quality objectives.
- Commit to satisfying applicable requirements.
- Commit to continual improvement of the QMS.
- Be approved and demonstrated by leadership.
- Be communicated, understood, applied, maintained, and available as appropriate.

Avoid a generic statement that employees cannot connect to their work. Review the policy when strategy, context, or stakeholder expectations materially change.

## 8. Quality objectives and planning

Set objectives at relevant functions and process levels. Each objective should have:

- Intended outcome and relationship to the quality policy.
- Measure, baseline, target, and due date.
- Owner and contributors.
- Planned actions and resources.
- Data source and measurement method.
- Review cadence and escalation thresholds.

Balance outcomes and leading indicators. Examples include customer journey success, on-time delivery, service availability, complaint recurrence, escaped defects, supplier performance, corrective-action effectiveness, process cycle time, and employee competence.

Do not select targets that encourage gaming, concealment, unsafe work, or local optimisation. Review interactions between cost, speed, quality, security, resilience, and customer outcomes.

## 9. Risks and opportunities

Use risk-based thinking throughout the process architecture. The 2026 edition of ISO 9001 distinguishes more clearly between managing risks and pursuing opportunities.

For each material process:

- Identify uncertainty that could prevent intended outcomes.
- Assess likelihood, impact, detectability, velocity, and existing controls as appropriate.
- Select treatment proportionate to the risk.
- Identify opportunities to improve value, capability, efficiency, resilience, or customer satisfaction.
- Integrate actions into operational plans rather than maintaining a disconnected register.
- Evaluate whether actions were effective.

Risk records should support decisions. Do not create elaborate scoring systems that obscure obvious priorities or imply false mathematical precision.

## 10. Roles, responsibilities, and authority

Define and communicate responsibility for:

- QMS integrity and coordination.
- Process ownership and performance.
- Product, service, and requirement approval.
- Customer communication and complaint resolution.
- Control of nonconforming outputs.
- Corrective action and effectiveness review.
- Document and record ownership.
- Supplier selection, monitoring, and escalation.
- Internal audit and auditor independence.
- Management review and risk acceptance.

The quality manager or QMS lead facilitates the system but does not own every quality outcome. Process owners remain responsible for their results and controls.

## 11. Competence, awareness, and organisational knowledge

Determine competence required for work affecting quality. Evaluate education, training, skills, and experience; close gaps; and verify that development actions were effective.

Maintain evidence proportionate to risk, such as role profiles, training records, supervised practice, certifications, peer assessment, demonstrations, and performance results.

People should understand:

- The quality policy and relevant objectives.
- Their contribution to QMS effectiveness and customer outcomes.
- Applicable procedures, controls, and escalation paths.
- Consequences of nonconforming work.
- Expected quality culture and ethical behaviour.

Identify knowledge required to operate processes and achieve conformity. Protect it from loss through documentation, pairing, succession, communities of practice, decision records, retrospectives, and cross-training. Review new knowledge needs when technology, products, regulations, or customer expectations change.

## 12. Resources and infrastructure

Provide and maintain resources needed for process operation and product/service conformity:

- People and specialist expertise.
- Facilities, equipment, software, networks, cloud services, and work environments.
- Monitoring and measurement resources.
- Communication and collaboration systems.
- Organisational knowledge and external sources.
- Time and budget for prevention, review, testing, maintenance, and improvement.

Control infrastructure changes and maintenance according to risk. Validate tools or measurement systems when incorrect results could lead to unacceptable decisions.

## 13. Customer and requirement management

Before committing to deliver, review:

- Explicit customer requirements.
- Requirements necessary for intended use even if unstated.
- Statutory, regulatory, security, privacy, accessibility, and contractual obligations.
- Differences from proposals or prior agreements.
- Organisational capability, capacity, dependencies, and schedule.
- Acceptance, support, maintenance, and change conditions.

Resolve ambiguity before acceptance. Control requirement changes and communicate them to affected functions and suppliers.

Manage customer communication for product information, enquiries, orders, changes, feedback, complaints, incidents, and customer property. Do not let sales commitments bypass feasibility and quality review.

## 14. Design and development control

Apply a lifecycle appropriate to product risk and complexity. Define:

- Stages, responsibilities, resources, and review points.
- Inputs: functional, performance, security, privacy, accessibility, safety, regulatory, and lessons-learned requirements.
- Design outputs that are complete enough for implementation, verification, validation, delivery, and support.
- Reviews, verification, validation, and acceptance.
- Configuration and change control.
- Interfaces with suppliers and operational teams.
- Required records and release evidence.

Review inputs for completeness, consistency, clarity, testability, and conflicts. Ensure outputs support procurement, production, service delivery, operation, and maintenance.

Detailed software verification and validation are defined in [software-quality-testing-governance.md](software-quality-testing-governance.md).

## 15. Supplier and externally provided process control

The company remains accountable for quality when work is outsourced.

Classify suppliers by impact and apply proportionate controls:

- Selection and due diligence.
- Clear requirements, service levels, acceptance criteria, and right-to-audit terms.
- Security, privacy, resilience, regulatory, and continuity obligations.
- Approval of critical changes and subcontractors.
- Incoming, integration, or service acceptance checks.
- Ongoing performance, incident, complaint, and risk monitoring.
- Corrective action and escalation.
- Exit, transition, data return/deletion, and continuity plans.

Evaluate total risk and performance, not only price. Maintain approved-supplier records and evidence supporting continued use.

## 16. Operational control

Plan and control the conditions needed to deliver conforming products and services:

- Current requirements and acceptance criteria.
- Suitable procedures, tools, infrastructure, and competent people.
- Process and output monitoring at appropriate stages.
- Identification, traceability, preservation, handling, and delivery controls.
- Customer or supplier property protection.
- Change control and post-delivery obligations.
- Release authority and retained evidence.

Where outputs cannot be fully verified later, validate and periodically revalidate the process itself. Examples can include specialised migrations, automated decision systems, destructive transformations, or services whose effect emerges only after delivery.

## 17. Documented information

Control documents and records according to their purpose, risk, and obligations.

### Documents that direct work

Policies, standards, procedures, plans, specifications, templates, and work instructions should have:

- Owner and approver.
- Purpose, scope, and intended audience.
- Unique identification and version.
- Review and approval before use.
- Effective date and review trigger or interval.
- Controlled access and distribution.
- Change history where useful.
- Withdrawal or clear marking of obsolete versions.

### Records that provide evidence

Records should be identifiable, authentic, legible, protected, retrievable, retained, and disposed of according to legal, contractual, operational, and privacy needs. Prevent unauthorised modification and define the system of record.

Do not document every action indiscriminately. Document what is necessary for reliable operation, knowledge, conformity, evidence, and improvement.

## 18. Measurement and analysis

Determine what must be monitored and measured, how, when, by whom, and how results will be evaluated.

Validate that measures are:

- Relevant to objectives and decisions.
- Defined consistently.
- Derived from reliable data.
- Timely enough for action.
- Segmented sufficiently to expose important variation.
- Protected against manipulation and inappropriate access.

Use trends, distributions, and causal investigation rather than isolated averages. Distinguish normal process variation from significant change before reacting.

Maintain calibrated or verified measurement equipment where physical measurement traceability matters. For software and analytical measurements, control formulas, queries, dashboards, source data, versioning, and access.

## 19. Customer satisfaction, feedback, and complaints

Monitor customer perceptions using a combination of direct and indirect evidence:

- Interviews, surveys, reviews, and advisory groups.
- Complaints, returns, cancellations, renewals, and churn.
- Support volume, escalation, and resolution experience.
- Product usage, journey success, reliability, and outcome measures.
- Contract performance and account feedback.

Survey scores alone are incomplete and can be biased. Analyse qualitative themes and operational behaviour.

Maintain a complaint process that acknowledges, investigates, resolves, communicates, records, and learns. Protect complainants from retaliation and consider accessibility, confidentiality, conflict of interest, and escalation. Repeated complaints should trigger systemic investigation.

## 20. Nonconforming outputs

Identify and control outputs that fail requirements so they are not unintentionally used or delivered.

Possible dispositions include:

- Correction and re-verification.
- Segregation, containment, suspension, or return.
- Customer notification.
- Acceptance under authorised concession.
- Reclassification or alternative use when permitted.
- Rejection, rollback, withdrawal, or destruction.

Record the nonconformity, affected output, actions, concessions, authority, and verification. Assess whether similar outputs, customers, time periods, or processes are affected.

In software, nonconforming outputs can include defective releases, inaccurate data, failed service levels, insecure configurations, incomplete documentation, or unfulfilled support commitments—not only source-code defects.

## 21. Correction, corrective action, and CAPA

Use terms precisely:

- **Correction:** fixes the detected nonconformity or immediate consequence.
- **Containment:** limits further impact while the issue is understood.
- **Corrective action:** removes or reduces the cause to prevent recurrence.
- **Preventive thinking:** identifies and treats risks before nonconformity; modern ISO 9001 integrates this through risk-based planning rather than a separate mandatory preventive-action procedure.

“CAPA” commonly refers to corrective and preventive action, especially in regulated industries. Use any terminology required by the applicable sector while preserving these distinctions.

A corrective-action process should:

1. Contain impact and correct the immediate issue.
2. Determine scope, significance, and reporting duties.
3. Investigate causes and contributing system conditions.
4. Search for similar actual or potential nonconformities.
5. Define proportionate actions, owners, and deadlines.
6. Implement and document the changes.
7. Verify completion.
8. Evaluate effectiveness after enough time or exposure.
9. Update risks, documents, training, controls, and QMS design as needed.

Do not close an action because a document changed or training was delivered. Confirm that recurrence risk was actually reduced.

## 22. Root-cause analysis

Select a method appropriate to complexity and impact:

- Five Whys for a narrow causal chain.
- Fishbone/Ishikawa categories for structured brainstorming.
- Fault-tree analysis for combinations leading to a failure.
- Timeline and change analysis for incidents.
- Causal mapping for interacting technical and organisational factors.
- Pareto analysis for recurring categories.

Avoid stopping at “human error,” “insufficient training,” or the last technical defect. Investigate why the system allowed the action, failed to detect it, or amplified its impact. Consider requirements, incentives, workload, interfaces, tools, approvals, environment, communication, and management decisions.

Not every minor defect requires formal root-cause analysis. Define risk-based triggers such as safety impact, major customer harm, regulatory breach, recurrence, widespread exposure, or ineffective prior action.

## 23. Internal audit

Create a risk-based audit programme covering QMS requirements, important processes, prior findings, significant changes, complaints, incidents, supplier performance, and weak results.

Audits should determine both conformity and effectiveness. An audit should not merely check that a document exists; it should examine whether the process achieves intended outcomes and controls risk.

Apply the ISO 19011 principles of integrity, fair presentation, due professional care, confidentiality, independence, evidence-based approach, and risk-based approach.

For each audit:

- Define objective, scope, criteria, methods, and team.
- Ensure auditor competence and sufficient independence from the work audited.
- Review relevant documents and prior evidence.
- Sample records, observe work, and interview participants.
- Record objective evidence and distinguish findings from recommendations.
- Communicate conclusions promptly.
- Assign correction and corrective action according to significance.
- Verify completion and effectiveness.

Audit sampling provides limited assurance. Do not describe an absence of sampled findings as proof that no problems exist.

Source: [ISO — ISO 19011:2026](https://www.iso.org/standard/19011)

## 24. Management review

Top management should review the QMS at planned intervals and after major changes or failures when needed.

Inputs should include:

- Status of previous actions.
- Context and interested-party changes.
- Customer satisfaction and feedback.
- Quality objectives and process performance.
- Product/service conformity and nonconformities.
- Audit, assessment, complaint, and incident results.
- Supplier performance.
- Resource, competence, infrastructure, and knowledge adequacy.
- Risk and opportunity treatment effectiveness.
- Corrective-action effectiveness.
- Improvement opportunities.

Outputs should record decisions and actions concerning improvement, QMS changes, resources, strategy, risks, and accountability. A dashboard presentation without decisions, owners, or follow-up is not an effective management review.

## 25. Continual improvement

Use multiple improvement sources:

- Objectives and process measures.
- Customer and employee feedback.
- Audits, nonconformities, incidents, and complaints.
- Supplier and partner feedback.
- Experiments, retrospectives, benchmarking, and innovation.
- Changes in technology, regulation, markets, and risk.

Apply a disciplined learning cycle such as Plan–Do–Check–Act:

1. **Plan:** understand the problem or opportunity, baseline performance, define the desired outcome, and choose an intervention.
2. **Do:** test or implement the change with appropriate controls.
3. **Check:** compare evidence with the expected outcome and identify unintended effects.
4. **Act:** standardise an effective change, adjust it, or stop it; retain learning.

Prioritise improvement by customer value, risk reduction, strategic alignment, feasibility, and learning—not by the number of ideas logged.

## 26. Certification and external assessment

Decide whether ISO 9001 certification supports customer expectations, procurement eligibility, risk reduction, or organisational discipline. Certification has costs for implementation, audit, maintenance, surveillance, and staff time.

If pursuing certification:

- Define the certification scope and sites honestly.
- Obtain the current standard and conduct a gap assessment.
- Operate the QMS long enough to generate evidence.
- Complete internal audits and management review.
- Correct material gaps before the certification audit.
- Select a competent certification body; verify relevant accreditation and sector experience.
- Plan for surveillance, recertification, and transition requirements.

Do not build two systems—one for work and another for auditors. The documented system should describe and improve actual operations.

## 27. Integration with other management systems

ISO management-system standards use a harmonised structure, enabling shared governance across quality, information security, privacy, environment, business continuity, occupational health and safety, and other disciplines.

Common elements can include:

- Context and interested parties.
- Policy and objectives.
- Roles and competence.
- Risk and opportunity processes.
- Documented information.
- Internal audits and management review.
- Nonconformity, corrective action, and improvement.

Integrate shared processes while preserving domain-specific expertise and evidence. A single risk register or audit programme can reduce duplication, but it must still meet each applicable standard’s requirements.

- Security, privacy, performance, accessibility, resilience, and recovery evidence.
- Release approval, deployment, monitoring, and rollback records.
- Defects, incidents, customer feedback, and corrective actions.

## 29. Common failure modes

- Treating certification as the objective instead of customer value and process effectiveness.
- Assigning “quality” to one team while process owners avoid accountability.
- Writing procedures that do not match actual work.
- Measuring activity volume rather than outcomes and risk.
- Conducting audits as checklist theatre.
- Closing corrective actions without effectiveness checks.
- Treating training as the default response to systemic design problems.
- Using “human error” as a root cause.
- Allowing sales or schedule pressure to bypass requirement and feasibility review.
- Evaluating suppliers only at onboarding.
- Keeping obsolete documents accessible at the point of use.
- Collecting customer scores without investigating behaviour and complaints.
- Holding management reviews that produce no decisions or actions.
- Optimising departments while cross-process failures continue.
- Creating excessive documentation that obscures essential controls.

## 30. Minimum controlled documentation

Maintain only what the organisation needs, including as applicable:

- QMS scope and process architecture.
- Quality policy and objectives.
- Roles, authorities, and process ownership.
- Applicable requirements, risks, and opportunities.
- Operational criteria, standards, and procedures.
- Competence and awareness evidence.
- Supplier approval and monitoring records.
- Product/service acceptance and release evidence.
- Measurement and customer-feedback results.
- Nonconformity, correction, corrective-action, and effectiveness records.
- Audit programme, reports, and follow-up.
- Management-review inputs, decisions, actions, and closure.
- Improvement portfolio and outcome evidence.

## 31. Suggested implementation order

### First 30 days

- Appoint an accountable executive sponsor and QMS lead.
- Define intended outcomes, business case, scope, and relevant interested parties.
- Map the highest-level value and support processes and assign owners.
- Identify critical customer, legal, contractual, and regulatory requirements.
- Draft the quality policy and initial objectives.
- Establish basic complaint, nonconformity, and corrective-action records.

### Days 31–90

- Define process inputs, outputs, controls, measures, records, and interactions.
- Establish document and record control.
- Assess risks, opportunities, competence, infrastructure, supplier controls, and measurement needs.
- Align actual operating procedures with QMS requirements.
- Begin monitoring objectives, customer feedback, process performance, and suppliers.
- Train process owners, auditors, and staff on their responsibilities.

### Months 4–12

- Operate the system and collect representative evidence.
- Conduct risk-based internal audits and close findings effectively.
- Hold management review and act on its decisions.
- Improve weak processes using measured outcomes and root-cause analysis.
- Integrate software, security, privacy, continuity, and other management systems.
- If certification is valuable, conduct a readiness assessment and engage a competent certification body.

## 32. QMS metrics

Use a balanced set of measures such as:

- Customer outcome, satisfaction, complaint, renewal, and churn trends.
- Product/service conformity and escaped nonconformities.
- Delivery reliability and service-level achievement.
- Process capability, cycle time, rework, and waste.
- Supplier conformity, incidents, and recovery.
- Corrective-action age, recurrence, and effectiveness.
- Audit findings by significance, recurrence, and closure quality.
- Quality-objective progress and overdue actions.
- Competence gaps and verified development effectiveness.
- Cost of poor quality, where it can be measured responsibly.

Interpret measures together. A fall in reported defects can indicate improvement, reduced use, weak detection, or suppressed reporting. Validate the story behind the number.
